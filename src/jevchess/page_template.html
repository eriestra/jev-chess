Jev at chess

TypeSafe's Jev 1.13 was given chess positions and asked for the best move, choosing from the complete list of legal moves. It saw nothing else. Stockfish 19 graded every choice. The test covers {{H_N}} positions from rated Lichess games, {{PZ_N}} Lichess puzzles and {{N_GAMES}} games against players of known strength.

The question Jev answers

Each move is one TypeSafe request with a single Choice question. The state is the board; the options are every legal move in standard algebraic notation (SAN), each with a plain description. Jev returns a probability for every option, and the player plays the most probable one. This is the request for the second position in the examples below, with the option list shortened for display:

{{REQUEST}}

The request carries piece placement, side to move, castling rights and the en passant square. It carries no move history, no evaluation and no search. Because every option is legal, Jev cannot make an illegal move; the test measures move selection alone. A Choice accepts up to 255 options, more than the 218 legal moves a chess position can have.

Move quality

Positions come from rated Lichess games played in January 2013, one position per game at a random point between move 5 and move 60. Stockfish 19 scores every legal move of each position, and each chosen move is graded by how much it loses against the best one. Sonnet 5 answered the first {{LLM_POS}} of these positions with the same request as text.

What Jev chooses

The capture-first rule is a control with no model: it plays a mating move if one exists, otherwise captures the most valuable piece it can, otherwise gives check, otherwise plays a random legal move. It does not look at whether the captured piece is defended.

Games

Stockfish can limit its strength to a chosen Elo, but not below 1,320. That setting is the anchor: its UCI_Elo scale was fitted to the CCRL Blitz rating list with games from the 8moves_v3 opening book at 120 seconds plus 1 second per move. All games here start from that book, and Stockfish 1320 at 120 + 1 is fixed at 1,320.

At this setting Stockfish picks its move from a depth-1 search and plays that pick however long it searches afterwards (search.cpp, Skill::time_to_pick). To keep the games short, the other Stockfish players run on a 10 + 0.1 second clock, and a {{VAL_N}}-game match between Stockfish 1320 on the two clocks measures the difference: on the fast clock it rates {{SF_FAST}} ({{SF_FAST_CI}}). Below the anchor, the scale is extended with reference players whose strength is measured here: Stockfish 1320 playing a random legal move 10%, 25%, 50% or 75% of the time, the capture-first rule, and a random mover. Each opening is played twice with colours reversed. Only Stockfish plays on a clock; Sonnet 5 plays against the anchor itself.

Method

Players

Jev: jev-latest (Jev 1.13.0), one Choice request per move as shown above, highest-probability option played. Sonnet 5: the same state, instruction and move list sent as text through the Claude Code CLI with default settings, asked to reply with one move from the list; a reply that names no legal move is counted and replaced by a random legal move in games. Capture-first rule and random legal move: no model. Stockfish 19: 1 thread, 16 MB hash, UCI_LimitStrength with UCI_Elo 1320; the anchor plays on a 120 + 1 second clock and the other Stockfish players on 10 + 0.1; the random-move variants replace Stockfish's move with a uniformly random legal move at the stated rate.

Grading

Stockfish 19 at full strength searches every legal move of a position to depth 12 (MultiPV over all moves). Scores are clipped to ±1,000 centipawns. Loss is the best move's score minus the chosen move's score. Accuracy and blunders use the Lichess definitions: win% = 50 + 50 · (2 / (1 + e−0.00368208 · cp) − 1); accuracy = 103.1668 · e−0.04354 · Δwin% − 3.1669; a blunder is a drop of 0.3 in winning chances (15 points of win%). On the 100 pilot positions, depth 12 and depth 16 rank moves alike (median Spearman correlation {{DC_RHO}}, same best move in {{DC_BEST}} of 100) and give Jev the same average loss ({{DC_12}} and {{DC_16}}).

Games and ratings

Openings are drawn from 8moves_v3.pgn (eight moves per side); Jev plays {{BOOK_N}} openings against each opponent, twice each with colours reversed. Games end by checkmate, stalemate, insufficient material, threefold repetition, the 50-move rule, or 400 plies (scored as a draw); Stockfish loses on time if its clock runs out. Ratings maximise the likelihood of all results under P(A beats B) = 1 / (1 + 10(RB − RA)/400), with draws as half points.

Limits

  • The game rating sits on the CCRL Blitz scale that Stockfish's UCI_Elo was fitted to in January 2023. It is not a FIDE or Lichess rating, and Stockfish 19 at the same setting may play somewhat differently from the version that was calibrated.
  • Ratings below 1,320 are carried down from the anchor through reference players that mix engine moves with random ones. Their play differs from human play at the same rating.
  • Jev sees each position without its history, so it cannot avoid or aim for a threefold repetition. Jev and Sonnet 5 play without a clock.
  • The held-out positions come from Lichess games of January 2013 (median player rating {{H_HUM_MED}}). Positions are graded at depth 12.
  • The games, positions and puzzles are public and may appear in Jev's training data. The move options carry facts computed by code: SAN marks checks and mates, and the descriptions name the captured piece.
  • One instruction wording was tested, in six input formats. Sonnet 5 was tested at its default effort through one client; other settings would give other numbers.
  • Sonnet 5 answered {{SH_N}} positions and {{PZ_LLM_N}} puzzles, fewer than Jev, so its intervals are wider.

Next steps

  • Next step: keep the legality and safety checks in code (for example, drop options that leave a piece to be taken for free) and give Jev the Choice among the moves that remain.
  • Next step: ask per-move Noul questions (“does this move leave the queen where it can be captured?”) and combine the probabilities in code, instead of one Choice over all moves.
  • Next step: add positions from games played after Jev 1.13's release, colour-flipped copies of every position, and a bare-coordinate option format (g1f3), so that a future gain can be told apart from memorisation or from reading the hints in the options.
  • Next step: re-run the same request set on each new Jev release; every request is stored, so the comparison needs only the new answers.
